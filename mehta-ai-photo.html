Man Sues AI Company Over Dispute About His Appearance in 1980

Raghav Mehta says an AI reconstruction of an old photograph made him less handsome than he remembers, although the photographs have introduced an incongruous complication.

By The Banana Times Staff · September 30, 2026

MUMBAI — Raghav Mehta has filed a lawsuit against Axiom Intelligence after the company's artificial-intelligence software recreated a photograph of him from 1980 in a way he says bears little resemblance to the man he remembers being.

Mehta said he became psychologically distressed after seeing the reconstruction, which gave him thicker hair, a sharper jawline and the kind of facial symmetry usually associated with men who are asked to stand near motorcycles in advertisements.

His objection, however, is that the AI version is not handsome enough.

“I know what I looked like,” Mehta said. “Everyone says use AI, and AI is not wrong. But I looked much better than that.”

The dispute became more complicated after Mehta submitted the original photograph as evidence. In it, he is wearing oversized glasses and a thin mustache, while his hair appears to have reached an independent agreement with gravity. Several people shown the two photographs have privately expressed difficulty identifying the precise direction of Mehta's complaint.

Mehta said his frustration peaked when he considered banging his computer to correct the image. According to a transcript included with the complaint, the company's chatbot advised him that banging the computer was unlikely to improve historical accuracy and could make the recreated image even more unmatched.

Axiom Intelligence offered Mehta a cash settlement and proposed naming a future image model “Raghav-4” in recognition of the dispute. Mehta rejected both offers, saying accuracy in the recreated photograph was more important to him than money or having an artificial-intelligence model bear his name.

The company has since announced that it will invest $3 billion in what it calls “historical facial accuracy,” an initiative intended to help its systems distinguish between what a person looked like, what surviving photographs show, and what that person has spent several decades remembering.

A company spokesperson said Axiom remains committed to resolving the matter but declined to say whether the new program would allow users to specify how handsome they believe they were in a particular decade.

Mehta remains unmoved.

“I don't want money,” he said. “I don't want a model named after me. I just want the truth.”
